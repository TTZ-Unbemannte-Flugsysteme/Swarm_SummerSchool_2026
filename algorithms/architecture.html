00 — Scope

What this document is for

The agreed scenario is leader-follower: one vehicle flies the mission, the others hold formation on it. You already have the hardware stack — ArduPilot on the flight controller, a Raspberry Pi running DroneBridge as the companion and telemetry bridge, Mission Planner on a PC — and all of it shares one Wi‑Fi network.

The good news is that leader-follower is not something this project has to invent. ArduPilot ships a FOLLOW flight mode that does exactly this, driven entirely by parameters. That decision shapes the rest of the design: the simulation's main job becomes proving the plumbing — that each follower actually receives the leader's position often enough and fast enough to hold station — rather than writing formation control from scratch.

Nothing here is implemented yet. It is meant to be read, argued with, and narrowed to one option before code lands in this repo.

01 — Bill of materials

What this pins down for the simulation

Several questions that were open in the earlier draft now have answers, which means the Gazebo model and SITL parameters can be specified rather than guessed:

  • Frame: quad in X layout — FRAME_CLASS = 1, FRAME_TYPE = 1. 450 mm diagonal puts each motor about 159 mm out in both axes from centre.
  • Propulsion: 2212 920 KV on 1045 props, 4S. The SDF model's thrust curve and the real one should agree, or FOLL_POS_P tuned in simulation will not transfer.
  • Power wiring: the ESCs are connected directly to the board, with every XT60 connector removed — none remain in the power path. That saves weight and space and deletes a failure point, and it is the arrangement the aircraft are actually built with, not a proposal. The trade is serviceability: swapping a motor or ESC in the field is now a desoldering job rather than an unplug.
  • Mass: the one number to measure rather than take from this page. An F450 built out like this typically lands somewhere around 1.5 kg all-up, but weigh a finished aircraft — the sim is only as good as this figure.
  • Every vehicle is a Copter, which keeps FOLLOW available on all of them.
  • Companion stack: Ubuntu Server with MAVROS and ROS 2, per the build documentation. Worth noting for Section 08 — a ROS 2 transport is much less of an imposition when ROS 2 is already the intended stack.
  • Pi ↔ FC link: /dev/serial0 at 921600 baud, with SERIALx_PROTOCOL = 2 and SERIALx_BAUD = 921 — ample for a high-rate position stream.
  • No compass is wired yet (COMPASS_DEV_ID = 0), which has direct consequences for the formation — see below.
The constraint worth internalising

Your GNSS choice sets the minimum safe formation spacing. FOLLOW steers to an offset from the leader's reported position, so the error that matters is the relative error between two independent receivers — not the datasheet's absolute figure. With metre-level non-RTK modules, offsets of a few metres put the aircraft inside each other's position uncertainty, and the formation will breathe in and out by roughly the same magnitude as the spacing you asked for.

Start deliberately loose — think tens of metres rather than single digits — measure how much the formation actually wanders in simulation and then in the air, and only tighten from evidence. Genuinely tight formation flight needs RTK or relative positioning, which is a hardware change, not a parameter change. Better to discover that now than after a close pass.

For a search-and-rescue sweep this lands softly: wide, loose spacing is what covers ground efficiently, so the constraint pushes the formation in the direction the mission wants anyway. It would only really hurt a tight display formation.

Consequence of no compass

The build shows COMPASS_DEV_ID = 0 — no magnetometer wired, with the I²C pads free. Heading therefore comes from the EKF's GNSS-based estimate, which is workable in forward flight but weak at hover and slow speed.

That rules out heading-relative offsets in practice. FOLL_OFS_TYPE should stay in the North-East-Down frame, which means the formation will not rotate as the leader turns — followers hold a fixed compass-aligned offset regardless of heading. Fine for a sweep pattern; surprising if you were expecting a V that pivots with the leader. If the M10 module has a magnetometer on board, wiring it to those free I²C pads is the cheapest capability upgrade available here.

02 — Baseline

The real platform, as it exists today

Each aircraft is a three-box stack, and the shape of it matters: the flight controller does attitude and position control, while two independent radios hang off separate serial ports — a Raspberry Pi 4 carrying the mission software, and a XIAO-ESP32-C6 running DroneBridge for ground telemetry. Both speak MAVLink over ordinary Wi‑Fi, and the ground PC is another client on the same medium. The only thing the leader-follower decision adds is a role per airframe.

03 — The mechanism

What leader-follower actually needs

ArduPilot's FOLLOW mode works by having the follower listen for the leader's MAVLink position messages and steer to a fixed offset from them. The leader is not configured as a leader at all — it just flies its mission and streams telemetry, exactly as it always has. Every bit of the formation behaviour lives in the followers' parameters.

That gives the simulation a sharp job to do. There is no formation algorithm to debug; there is a delivery problem to prove: each follower's flight controller must receive GLOBAL_POSITION_INT from the leader, continuously, at a few Hz, with low enough latency that the follower is not chasing a stale position.

The parameters that define the formation

ParameterWhat it doesFor this setup

What this forces on the rest of the design

  • Distinct system IDs. Every vehicle needs its own SYSID_THISMAV, in simulation and on the real aircraft, or the followers cannot tell anyone apart.
  • The leader's stream has to reach the followers. Not just the GCS. On the leader, the position stream rate on the link that reaches the followers must actually be set — a link streaming at a fraction of a Hz will produce visibly laggy, sawing formation flight.
  • No collision avoidance between followers. FOLLOW holds an offset; it does not know the other followers exist. Separation is entirely a property of the offsets you choose.
  • A leader-loss behaviour has to be decided. If the stream stops, the follower stops receiving updates. What it should then do — hold, return, land — is a design decision, not a default to inherit silently.
04 — Resolved

The old blocker is gone. Here is what replaces it.

Earlier drafts of this document treated one question as a blocker: whether DroneBridge was running in wifibroadcast mode, injecting raw 802.11 frames point-to-point, which would have meant followers could never hear the leader directly. The assembled hardware settles it.

Settled by the build

DroneBridge runs on a XIAO-ESP32-C6, not on the Raspberry Pi — the ESP32 variant, which bridges MAVLink over standard Wi‑Fi. There is no monitor mode and no raw injection anywhere in the stack. Everything is ordinary IP on a shared network, so any node can address any other node, and drone-to-drone delivery of the leader's position is a routing question rather than a physics one.

What the build also shows is that each flight controller has two independent serial links, and they are doing different jobs: the ESP32 is described as telemetry-only to the ground station, while the Pi has its own UART at 921600 baud for the mission software. That separation is useful, and it suggests where the swarm traffic belongs.

Put the swarm plane on the Pi network

The leader's position should reach the followers Pi to Pi over Wi‑Fi, with each follower's Pi injecting it into its own flight controller through that 921600 baud link. Three reasons:

  • It leaves the telemetry link alone. The ESP32 bridge keeps doing the one job it is built for. Formation traffic and ground telemetry stop competing for the same radio, and a busy GCS session cannot starve the thing keeping the formation together.
  • The bandwidth is already there. 921600 baud on the Pi link is far more headroom than a position stream needs, where a telemetry-grade link would have to be rationed.
  • It is the layer you control. The Pi is where you can rate-limit, timestamp, log, and deliberately degrade the stream for testing. An ESP32 bridge is a fixed appliance by comparison.

The open questions that remain are smaller and concrete: whether the ESP32 radios and the Pi radios join the same SSID or separate networks, whether the Pis get static addresses or discover each other, and whether the ESP32 acts as an access point or joins an existing one. Those are configuration decisions, not architectural ones.

06 — Proposed system

Simulation architecture

One Gazebo world holds the physics for every vehicle. Each vehicle gets its own SITL process and its own companion-agent process — one per drone, mirroring one Pi per drone — and all of them meet on a virtual network layer standing in for the shared Wi‑Fi. The leader-follower relationship shows up as parameters inside the follower SITL instances, not as extra infrastructure.

07 — The main decision

Where the formation logic lives

This is now the real fork in the design, and it matters more than the transport question that follows it. The formation target for each follower can be computed in one of two places: inside the follower's own flight controller, or in a coordinator process that sends it explicit position targets.

Option A is what FOLLOW mode already is: no code to write, no process to keep alive, and the formation degrades gracefully because each follower is autonomous once it can hear the leader. Its ceiling is that the offset is essentially fixed — the formation shape is a set of parameters, not a plan.

Option B buys real flexibility: formations that change mid-flight, per-follower tasking, spacing that reacts to what the other followers are doing. It costs a process that becomes a single point of failure for the entire swarm, and it puts every correction at the mercy of link latency. Mission Planner also has a swarm screen that implements roughly this pattern, which is worth opening before writing any coordinator of your own — what it can do varies by version.

08 — Transport

Getting the leader's position to the followers

09 — Recommendation

FOLLOW mode over mavlink-router

Proposed path

Formation logic in the flight controller (Option A). It is already in the firmware you are running, it needs no code, and the FOLL_* values you tune against Gazebo are the same values you will type into Mission Planner on the real aircraft. It also fails well: a follower that can hear the leader keeps its station whether or not the ground station is alive. Reach for a coordinator later, and only if you hit something the offsets genuinely cannot express.

Transport via mavlink-router (Option A). Leader-follower makes this a stronger pick than it was before the pattern was settled: you need the leader's stream delivered to specific followers, and a router config states that in one place instead of leaving it to whatever every node happens to overhear. It also gives you one host to attach tc netem to, which is how you will answer the question that actually matters — how far the link can degrade before the formation stops holding.

Run the formation traffic over the Pi network, not the telemetry bridge. The build gives every aircraft two independent links (Section 04), and the leader's position belongs on the Pi one — more headroom, and the ESP32 bridge stays dedicated to ground telemetry rather than competing with the thing holding the formation together.

On ROS 2: since MAVROS is already the planned companion stack, Option C is not the imposition this document previously called it. The nuance is that FOLLOW needs raw GLOBAL_POSITION_INT arriving at the flight controller, which is a MAVLink forwarding job rather than a pub/sub one. The clean arrangement is both: mavlink-router moving the leader's stream to each follower's FC, with a local UDP endpoint alongside it for MAVROS to attach to. The mission software gets ROS 2; the formation keeps a path that does not depend on it.

10 — Operator interface

Cameras, telemetry, and flying the leader by hand

Everything above describes a simulation you start and then watch. This section describes turning it into one you operate: a page per session carrying each drone's telemetry, a video feed from each, and direct keyboard control of the leader — so the formation can be demonstrated on demand rather than only measured by a script. Each of the three is a different path through the same processes, and in each one the ordering is the design, so they are drawn as sequence diagrams rather than as boxes.

Telemetry — the path that already exists

This one is mostly built. A reader thread per drone owns one UDP endpoint, keeps the latest state behind a lock, and the page polls a JSON snapshot. Worth drawing anyway, because the next two paths attach to the same server and inherit its rules — one owner per port, newest value wins, nothing assumed that can be read.

Flying the leader from the keyboard

The leader is the only vehicle a human needs to touch: the followers are already a function of it. Control goes through GUIDED velocity setpoints rather than RC_CHANNELS_OVERRIDE, and that choice is about authority, not convenience. An override actively fights a transmitter for the same channels; a mode-based setpoint loses to the transmitter the instant someone flips the mode switch, which is the behaviour you want the day this is pointed at a real aircraft.

Video from each drone

Each vehicle model gets a camera sensor publishing on its own gz-transport topic. The dashboard subscribes with the Python bindings, JPEG-encodes frames with OpenCV, and serves them as multipart/x-mixed-replace — the ancient MJPEG trick, which for three small feeds over loopback needs no negotiation, no signalling server and no client-side library. WebRTC would cut latency and is the right answer for video off a real aircraft over Wi‑Fi; it is the wrong answer for a localhost demo.

What this costs — measured, and not what was predicted

This section originally predicted that three rendered camera sensors would be the expensive part, and that a real-time factor below 1.0 would corrupt the flight measurements. Both halves turned out to be wrong, so here are the numbers instead. On this machine, three drones with the Gazebo GUI run at RTF 0.66 with no cameras at all and RTF 0.61 with three of them at 320×240 and 10 Hz (ten samples each). The cameras cost about 7 % relative; the low baseline is three SITL processes and the GUI, and it was already there.

The second correction matters more. Because the plugin is lock-stepped, SITL and Gazebo share one clock, so a low RTF does not corrupt the simulated flight — it stays self-consistent, and the station-keeping numbers remain valid. What a low RTF actually costs is wall-clock time, and it makes anything measured in wall-clock correspond to less sim time than it appears to: the sensor's 10 Hz is 10 Hz of sim time, which is why the dashboard measured 6.3 fps at RTF 0.63 and 9.9 fps once the machine was less loaded. The same applies to the browser's command loop and its deadman, both of which are wall-clock and therefore conservative here rather than dangerous.

The acceptance test is still worth running — SWARM_CAMERAS=0 flyit 3 builds the identical world without camera sensors, so the comparison takes two runs — but on this hardware the cameras are not what to cut if the simulation feels slow. Closing the Gazebo GUI is.

What this changes — the dashboard stops being read-only

One failure mode found while building this is worth writing down, because it looks like a bug in the formation and is not. If the leader descends and lands while the followers are still in FOLLOW, they dutifully follow it to the ground and stay armed there — and ArduPilot then refuses a fresh GUIDED takeoff for a vehicle that is armed and already on the ground. The take-off button therefore force-disarms anything sitting armed at zero altitude before arming it again, and every arm and take-off now waits for its COMMAND_ACK and reports the result on the page. That last part is the real fix: the first version failed completely silently, leaving vehicles on the ground while the UI looked like it had worked.

Until now nothing in the web UI could touch a vehicle; the page was a window. Step 01 of Fig. 6 changes that, and a browser tab becomes a flight control. In SITL the stakes are zero, but the stated goal of this repo is that its code and parameters transfer to real F450s, so the gate belongs in the design now rather than bolted on later: bind to loopback only, require an explicit take-control action instead of live-by-default keys, show on screen who currently holds authority, and keep the deadman short. On a real aircraft the FrSky transmitter must win every contest, which is the argument for GUIDED setpoints over RC override made above.

11 — Autonomous inspection

Pick a point, sweep an area, find what is on the road, look at it

Manual control answers “fly there”. This section answers the two questions that follow it: go here, chosen by clicking the map, and cover all of this and tell me what you found. Both reduce to the same primitive — a position target for the leader — because the followers are already a function of the leader. That is the dividend of putting the formation logic in the flight controller: a survey plan has exactly one vehicle in it, no matter how many are flying.

One click, one target

The map is drawn leader-centred, but a clicked target is stored as an absolute latitude and longitude and re-projected every frame. Storing an offset instead would make the target slide across the ground as the leader closed on it. Position targets go out as SET_POSITION_TARGET_GLOBAL_INT, whose 1e7 integer fields are exact; MAV_CMD_DO_REPOSITION is the obvious alternative and carries latitude as a float32 command parameter, which quantises a position to about a metre at these latitudes.

Finding the accident

There is nothing to find in the stock world, so the generator stages something: two cars and some debris on the runway centreline, in saturated red and yellow. Nothing else in this world is either colour — asphalt grey, markings white, grass green, sky pale — which is what makes a plain hue threshold a legitimate detector here rather than a toy. It is not a vehicle detector, and the day this points at real video it has to be replaced outright.

The interesting half is not detection but location. The camera looks forward and down, so a blob near the top of the frame sits near the horizon, where a small angular error becomes an enormous ground error. The projection therefore reports its own reliability and refuses to range anything shallower than 12° below horizontal, returning a bearing instead.

Aiming every camera at what you found

Once something is on the map, the useful next question is “let me look at it”. The cameras are bolted to the airframes — no gimbals, matching the real F450s — so aiming a camera means yawing the aircraft. That turns out to be free: FOLL_OFS_TYPE=0 puts the formation offsets in the NED frame, so the geometry does not rotate with heading and yaw is an unused degree of freedom. The swarm can stare at a fixed point on the ground while still flying wherever the leader goes.

What it does cost is FOLLOW mode, and finding that out is most of the story. ArduPilot's FOLLOW owns the yaw controller: it acks MAV_CMD_CONDITION_YAW and then ignores it, and FOLL_YAW_BEHAVE offers only “face the leader”, “copy the leader” and “face the direction of flight” — none of which is “face that point over there”. Copying the leader's heading nearly works, and fails exactly where it matters: measured with the swarm near the wreck, the three drones wanted 0°, 16° and 344°, a 32° spread, and up close the spread exceeded what a 69° lens can absorb entirely.

So while a point is being watched, the followers move to GUIDED and the dashboard holds their stations from the ground — Option B from Section 07, for exactly as long as the cameras are aimed, with their FOLL_* parameters left untouched so the handback is exact.

What the hand-off actually costs

Holding formation from the ground instead of in the flight controller is a real trade, and now a measured one. With the velocity feed-forward in place, a leader hand-flown at 6 m/s kept its followers within 1.0–1.6 m of station while all three cameras stayed within ±1.6° of their individual bearings — comparable to the 0.3–2.8 m that flight-controller FOLLOW held during a survey. Without the feed-forward it was 25 m. The difference that remains is not accuracy but dependency: while watching, the formation exists because the ground station is talking. If the dashboard or the link stops, the followers hold their last commanded position — safe, but no longer following. That is the whole Option A versus Option B argument, and it is why watching is a temporary mode with an explicit hand-back rather than the default.

Measured against the answer key

worlds/scene.json records where the accident was actually staged, which makes this testable rather than merely demonstrable. Flying the highway survey at 30 m, the swarm located it to 1.8 m; the wider area survey at 35 m, with 148 sightings from two drones, to 2.6 m. The negative control matters just as much: with SWARM_ACCIDENT=0 the generator stages nothing, and a full survey over the same clear road reported zero detections — so the detector is keying on the wreck and not on asphalt, markings or grass.

LOITER is not a hold — the bug this section cost

Every early survey ended with the swarm sitting on the ground, with no failsafe, no warning and nothing in the flight log but a mode change. The cause was a single line: on completion the leader was handed to LOITER. LOITER is a pilot-assisted mode — it holds position horizontally but takes its climb rate from the throttle stick, and in SITL that stick reads 1000 µs, which is full descent. Every “hold here” in this repo was quietly commanding a landing. The fix is to hold the current position as a GUIDED target, which needs no RC input at all; position targets, unlike velocity targets, do not expire, so one message does it. Worth remembering before any of this is pointed at an aircraft whose transmitter happens to be switched off.

12 — If this is approved

Proposed repo layout

Structured so companion/ ships to a real Pi unchanged, and both the formation and the network topology live in config rather than in code.

The first milestone worth aiming at is deliberately small: two vehicles, one leader and one follower, holding a fixed offset in Gazebo with Mission Planner watching both. Everything after that — a third vehicle, formation shapes, link degradation — is a variation on a loop that already closes.
