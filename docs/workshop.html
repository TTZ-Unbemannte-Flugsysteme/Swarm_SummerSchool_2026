01 — The system

What you are building

Three drones, each one a stack of three processes: a vehicle model inside Gazebo, an ArduPilot flight-code process, and a MAVProxy that copies the resulting MAVLink stream to everything that wants it. The formation itself is not a program — it is a handful of parameters inside each follower's flight controller.

02 — The concept students most often get wrong

Software in the loop, hardware in the loop, and real flight

“Simulation” is not one activity. It is a ladder, and each rung replaces one more simulated layer with a real one. Knowing which rung you are standing on tells you exactly which class of bug you are able to find — and which you are still blind to.

QuestionSITLthis repoHILreal boardReal flight
Is my formation logic right?yesyesyes
Are my ports and message routing right?yesyesyes
Does the firmware fit and run on the board?noyesyes
Is the board fast enough in real time?noyesyes
Is my wiring and power sound?nopartlyyes
Do two real GNSS receivers agree?nonoyes
Cost of getting it wrongrestart a scripta bench sessionan aircraft
The trap to warn them about

Our followers hold 0.0 m of station error. Students read that as success; it is the opposite. SITL gives both vehicles one perfect shared position truth, so the number measures the plumbing and nothing else. Real aircraft carry independent metre-class GNSS receivers, and it is the relative error between two of them that sets how tightly you may fly. Teach the number and the caveat in the same breath, or the first outdoor flight teaches it for you.

03 — Configuration

Why IP and port decide whether any of this works

Almost every confusing failure in a day like this comes back to one of two things: two programs fighting over a port, or a packet going to an address nobody is listening on. Both fail quietly. Neither raises an error, and both look like a flaky link.

One port, one owner

A UDP socket is not a broadcast. When two processes bind the same port, the operating system does not duplicate the traffic — it hands each datagram to one of them. Each program sees a random fraction of the stream, and the symptom is a vehicle that answers every other request, or a test that passes twice and fails once.

That is why every consumer here gets a port of its own and MAVProxy does the copying. On the real aircraft mavlink-router does the same job for the same reason.

Port for drone iSole ownerWhy it is separate
9002 + 10iGazebo ↔ SITLFlight dynamics, not MAVLink. Each vehicle model hard-codes one port, which is why the world generator clones the model per drone.
14550 + 10irelay.pyCarries the leader's position to the followers. Load-bearing: kill it and the formation stops.
14551 + 10itest scriptsSo a measurement run never competes with the relay for packets.
14552 + 10ithe dashboardAlso the only write path: manual control sends on this socket.
14553 + 10iMission PlannerA GCS expects to own its link. Sharing it with a script breaks both.
14554 + 10iflight_log.pyReads every vehicle's messages without disturbing anything else.

And the address

Everything in this simulation is on one machine, so everything belongs on 127.0.0.1. Two consequences are worth stating to a room:

  • Binding a control endpoint to 0.0.0.0 exposes it to the whole network. In a classroom that means anyone present can command your drones. The dashboard binds loopback only, deliberately.
  • Gazebo does not use a fixed address at all — it discovers peers by multicast, and chooses a network interface to do it on. It can choose a dead one.
A real failure from this build — worth telling as a story

The camera topics were listed correctly by gz topic -l. The subscriber connected without error. Not one frame ever arrived. Buried in the Gazebo log was Exception sending a multicast message: Network is unreachable — a disconnected ethernet port and an idle docker0 were enough for discovery to pick an interface that could not carry it. The fix is one line, GZ_IP=127.0.0.1, and it is in scripts/env.sh so nobody has to rediscover it.

The lesson generalises: when a data path is silent but nothing reports an error, suspect the address before you suspect the code.

Configuration files, and what belongs in them

Three kinds of setting, kept apart on purpose:

# scripts/env.sh    - addresses, ports, paths. Where things are.
relay_port()  { echo $((14550 + 10 * $1)); }
export GZ_IP="${GZ_IP:-127.0.0.1}"

# config/sitl/f450.parm  - what the aircraft IS. Frame, battery.
FRAME_CLASS   1
FRAME_TYPE    1

# config/follow/trail_20m.param  - what the formation SHOULD DO.
FOLL_ENABLE   1
FOLL_SYSID    1
FOLL_OFS_X  -20

The split matters because the middle and bottom files transfer to the real aircraft unchanged — you type those same values into Mission Planner. Only the top file is simulation-specific. Mixing them is how a simulation-only setting ends up on a flying drone.

04 — One command, in slow motion

What flyit actually does

Students will type one command and see a lot of output. This is what is underneath it, in order. Two steps are worth pausing on: the lock-step loop in the middle, and the heartbeat wait near the end.

$ flyit 3              # three drones in Gazebo
$ flyit --no-gazebo    # physics only, starts far faster
$ flyit --stop         # shut everything down
$ SWARM_ACCIDENT=0 flyit 3   # the detector's negative control

Where we are going

By the end of this session you will have three simulated drones flying a formation, a camera on each, and a browser page that flies the leader and reports what the swarm found on the ground.

  • Nothing here needs a drone. It all runs on one laptop.
  • Everything is measured: each step prints PASS or FAIL.
  • The parameters you tune here are the ones you type into the real aircraft.
Say: Open with the screenshot in the README. People need to see the destination before the parts list.
Slide 02 / 16

Why simulate at all

A mistake in a formation is not one broken aircraft. Followers chase the leader, so a bad offset or a lost link is a fleet-wide event.

  • An F450 costs money; a session costs a day.
  • Failures you must rehearse — lost leader, brown-out, bad offset — are exactly the ones too dangerous to try outdoors.
  • In simulation you can kill the link mid-flight on purpose. We do, and it is the most useful test in the repo.
Say: Ask the group: what happens to two followers if the leader's Pi browns out? That is the UBEC single point of failure in the hardware list.
Slide 03 / 16

Three ways to test, only one of them is cheap

Simulation is not one thing. There are three rungs, and each swaps one more simulated layer for a real one.

  • SITL — software in the loop. Everything is software on the PC.
  • HIL — hardware in the loop. The real flight controller, a fake world.
  • Real flight — everything real, one shot at a time.

Each rung only catches the bugs that live in the layer it just made real.

Say: Point at Figure 2. Orange is real, teal is simulated. Do not let anyone leave thinking SITL proves the aircraft flies.
Slide 04 / 16

What SITL cannot tell you

  • Whether your solder joints hold.
  • Whether the UBEC survives a loaded Pi.
  • Whether the real GNSS receivers agree with each other.
  • Whether the airframe you built matches the model you flew.

Our followers hold 0.0 m station error. That number is a warning, not a triumph — SITL has no GNSS noise, so both vehicles share one perfect truth.

Say: This slide is the honesty slide. Students who believe 0.0 m will be surprised outdoors. Relative GNSS error is what sets minimum safe spacing.
Slide 05 / 16

The one thing that breaks everything: ports

A UDP port has exactly one owner. Two programs bound to the same port do not share the traffic — they steal packets from each other, each seeing a random half.

This does not raise an error. It looks like a flaky link, a vehicle that half-responds, a test that fails intermittently.

Say: This is the single most common thing that will go wrong today. Say it twice.
Slide 06 / 16

The port map

One SITL process, one MAVProxy, five consumers — each with its own port, each port ten higher for the next drone.

          scripts    14551 + 10i   leader_follower.py
          dashboard  14552 + 10i   the web page
          GCS        14553 + 10i   Mission Planner

Slide 07 / 16

IP matters too, and fails more quietly

Everything here is on one machine, so everything should be on 127.0.0.1.

  • Bind to 0.0.0.0 and your drone control is on the room's Wi-Fi.
  • Gazebo discovers peers by multicast, and picks an interface to do it on. A disconnected ethernet port was enough to make it choose a dead one.

Symptom: camera topics listed fine and no frame ever arrived. Fix: GZ_IP=127.0.0.1.

Say: A real bug from this build. The topic existed, the subscriber existed, and nothing moved. Worth telling as a story.
Slide 08 / 16

Anatomy of one drone

Gazebo owns the physics. SITL is the real ArduPilot firmware compiled for your laptop. MAVProxy copies one MAVLink stream to five places.

Multiply by three, add ten to every port. That is the entire system.

Say: Figure 1. Ask where the flight code runs. The answer 'the same code as the real drone' is the point of SITL.
Slide 09 / 16

Lock-step: the sim clock is not your clock

Gazebo and SITL share one clock. If the renderer falls behind, time itself slows down — the flight stays correct, it just takes longer in the room.

  • Measured here: RTF 0.61–0.66 with three drones and the GUI open.
  • So a sensor set to 10 Hz delivered 6.3 frames a second of wall time.
  • Your stopwatch and the log will disagree. The log is right.
Say: Counter-intuitive and worth dwelling on: low real-time factor does not corrupt the physics under lock-step.
Slide 10 / 16

One command

$ flyit 3

Clears the last run, generates a world with one model per drone, starts Gazebo, starts three SITL instances, waits for a real heartbeat from each, then opens the dashboard.

Readiness means a heartbeat arrived — not that a port is open. An open port proves nothing.

Slide 11 / 16

Leader and followers

The formation is not a program. It is three parameters inside each follower's flight controller.

FOLL_ENABLE  1        # follow someone
FOLL_SYSID   1        # specifically drone 1
FOLL_OFS_X  -25       # 25 m behind
FOLL_OFS_Y  ±20       # 20 m to the side

Set those, switch to FOLLOW, and the follower does the rest on its own.

Say: Emphasise: no ground station is in the loop. A follower that can hear the leader keeps station even if your laptop dies.
Slide 12 / 16

The leader's position has to arrive

FOLLOW needs the leader's GLOBAL_POSITION_INT to reach each follower's flight controller. relay.py carries it.

It forwards the raw bytes, because rewriting the sender's system ID would stop it matching FOLL_SYSID and the formation would silently never form.

Say: On the aircraft this same script runs on the Pi. Same code, different transport.
Slide 13 / 16

Prove it, do not trust it

$ python3 scripts/leader_follower.py --vehicles 3 --kill-relay-at 48

Arms, climbs, forms up, flies the leader 120 m — then kills the relay on purpose.

  • While relaying: error stays near zero.
  • Relay dead: the follower freezes and the leader flies on alone, 120 m away.

That second half is the real result. It proves the formation is built on the relayed position and nothing else.

Say: Negative controls are the habit to teach. Anyone can show a thing working.
Slide 14 / 16

Fly it yourself

In the browser: Arm + take off all, then Followers → FOLLOW, then Take control.

  • W A S D north / west / south / east, R / F climb, Q / E yaw.
  • Let go of the key and the leader stops. Close the tab and it stops.

The page sends a velocity setpoint five times a second. Stop talking to ArduPilot and it stops moving — that is the safety feature, not a limitation.

Say: Let everyone fly. Then ask what happens if the wifi drops mid-command. The deadman is the answer.
Slide 15 / 16

Find something

Inspect the highway flies the survey. A staged accident sits on the runway; the cameras look for it, and the dashboard reports a position and a photo.

  • Located to 1.8–2.6 m against the known ground truth.
  • Run it again with SWARM_ACCIDENT=0: zero detections. That is the control.

The detector is a colour threshold. It works because nothing else here is red — it would not survive real video for a minute.

Say: Good moment to discuss what a demo proves and what it does not.
Slide 16 / 16

What will go wrong today

  • Two things on one port — flaky, intermittent, no error. Check with check_vehicles.py.
  • Armed on the ground — ArduPilot refuses a fresh takeoff. ./flyit --stop and start again.
  • Arm refused — wait a minute. EKF and GNSS need to settle.
  • No camera frames — GZ_IP=127.0.0.1.

When something misbehaves, read .run/latest/flight_log.txt before touching anything.

Say: Close here. Reading the log first is the habit that will save them the most time.
